About the Topic

Cerebral Palsy:

Cerebral Palsy is a group of brain disorders/conditions that affect body movement and basic muscle coordination. This group of conditions permanently affects body movement and muscle coordination. This condition typically forms before birth, during its time in the womb when it is facing damage to the developing brain. Although it develops before birth symptoms tend to show in early infancy or preschool years. The function and impact of cerebral palsy depends and varies between each person.

Causes:

  • Damage to the brain’s white matter
  • Gene changes that interrupt development of the brain
  • Bleeding in the brain
  • Lack of oxygen
  • Maternal infections that affect the fetus
  • Strokes, which stop the blood flow to the baby’s brain
  • Infant infections that cause swelling and disruption to the developing brain
  • TBI (traumatic brain injury) often caused by falling, physical trauma, or accidents

Symptoms:

  • Spasticity (stiff/tight muscles and unnecessary and exaggerated reflexes)
  • Ataxia (lack of muscle coordination and balance when trying to perform voluntary acts of movement)
  • Dystonia (also known as a tremor, jerky movements and actions you can’t control)
  • Unusual gait (different walking style and trouble walking)
  • Stiff or floppy muscle tone
  • Delays in movement milestones (lifting their head, rolling or sitting, walking, etc)
  • Difficulty with fine motor skills (writing or buttoning a shirt)

Definition:

“Transgender” is a term for a person’s gender identity who is different from their sex assigned at birth.

Statistics:

In the United States about 2.14 Million adults (18+) identify as transgender, this is about 0.8% of the adult population in the U.S. About 724,000 teens between the ages of 13-17 identify as transgender, this is about 3.3% of the youth population in the united states.

Stigma & Society

Stigma Definition

Stigma has a lot of different meanings and can be interpreted as many different things. When people say “that's [ex: disability] stigma,” some people may agree to disagree.

But according to the Link and Phelian definition of stigma, it states, “stigma exists when the following interrelated components converge. In the first component, people distinguish and label human differences. In the second, dominant cultural beliefs link labeled persons to undesirable characteristics—to negative stereotypes. In the third, labeled persons are placed in distinct categories so as to accomplish some degree of separation of “us” from “them.” In the fourth, labeled persons experience status loss and discrimination that lead to unequal outcomes. Finally, stigmatization is entirely contingent on access to social, economic, and political power that allows the identification of differentness, the construction of stereotypes, the separation of labeled persons into distinct categories, and the full execution of disapproval, rejection, exclusion, and discrimination.”

Link and Phelian propose that stigma is a layered concept, these layers overlapping and coexisting with one another to make it be defined as “stigma.” The first layer being us humans noticing differences from ourselves to others. The second layer is a cultural significance that impacts the way we perceive certain things. The third layer is a way of categorizing people based on their characteristics. The fourth layer is based on human experiences and how the discrimination they face affects them and labels them as a whole. Lastly, the fifth layer contributes to many other broad layers. In the end, stigma is a system of overlapping layers that are connected and can have a huge toll on someone’s health, life, and overall social status.

Cerebral Palsy Stigma and Discrimination

Cerebral Palsy Is Contagious

A stigma/stereotype of cerebral palsy is that it can be contagious. This is not true and even though there is data and real logical evidence to prove this, there is still a stigma stating that cerebral palsy can be passed along if you come close to someone who has it.

Cerebral palsy is mostly developed due to damage in the developing brain when the baby is a fetus, so this cannot be true. This stereotype often hurts people with cerebral palsy because it makes them feel isolated due to many people viewing their condition as an illness that can spread around.

People With CP Are Intellectually Disabled

The stigma that people with CP are not capable of learning or intelligent enough is highly active and harmful. Due to this view, many people with cerebral palsy are demotivated and struggle with self-worth when others put the idea into their heads that they are incapable of intelligence.

This can contribute to many mental health issues including depression and anxiety.

Transgender Stigma in the United States

Transgender stigma and discrimination in the United States is unfortunately an extremely common occurrence. A study by Jamie M. Grant shares how transgender individuals have encountered many types of unwelcoming environments and pressure while in medical environments.

“A national study of over 6000 transgender adults found that 28% had experienced harassment in medical settings, 19% were refused care, and 2% experienced violence in their doctor's office” (Grant et al., 2011).

Stereotypes and Their Effects

Discrimination Against Transgender People Due to Lack of Acceptance

One of the ways transgender individuals face stigma is in the workplace. Some employers still have yet to accept people who identify as transgender, especially in professional environments.

These employers may view transgender individuals as “unprofessional,” causing gender non-conforming individuals to lose jobs or receive fewer benefits than cisgender individuals.

A study by Jamie M. Grant states, “Forty-seven percent (47%) said they had experienced an adverse job outcome, such as being fired, not hired or denied a promotion because of being transgender…”

This means almost half of the people surveyed experienced workplace discrimination because of their gender identity rather than their actual skills or abilities.

Research also shows that many private insurers attempt to exclude coverage for gender affirming care by labeling it as “cosmetic,” “pre-existing,” or “medically unnecessary” (Khan, 2013).

In addition, insufficient provider training creates discomfort and sometimes hostile treatment for transgender patients (Lurie, 2005; Poteat et al., 2013).
