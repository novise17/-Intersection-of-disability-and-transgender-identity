About the Topic

Cerebral Palsy:

Cerebral Palsy is a group of brain disorders/conditions that affect body movement and basic muscle coordination. This group of conditions permanently affects body movement and muscle coordination. This condition typically forms before birth, during its time in the womb when it is facing damage to the developing brain. Although it develops before birth symptoms tend to show in early infancy or preschool years. The function and impact of cerebral palsy depends and varies between each person.

Causes:

Cerebral palsy is typically caused by damage to the growing brain during the mother's pregnancy. Some of these may include but not limited to:

  • Damage to the brain's white matter
  • Gene changes that interrupt development of the brain
  • Bleeding in the brain
  • Lack of oxygen
  • Maternal infections that affect the fetus
  • Strokes, which stop the blood flow to the baby's brain
  • Infant infections that cause swelling and disruption to the developing brain
  • TBI (traumatic brain injury) often caused by falling, physical trauma, or accidents

Symptoms:

  • Spasticity (stiff/tight muscles and unnecessary and exaggerated reflexes)
  • Ataxia (lack of muscle coordination and balance when trying to perform voluntary acts of movement)
  • Dystonia (also known as a tremor, jerky movements and actions you can't control)
  • Unusual gait (different walking style and trouble walking)
  • Stiff or floppy muscle tone
  • Delays in movement milestones (lifting their head, rolling or sitting, walking, etc)
  • Difficulty with fine motor skills (writing or buttoning a shirt)

Stigma & Society

Stigma Definition

Stigma has a lot of different meanings and can be interpreted as many different things. When people say "that's [ex: disability] stigma," some people may agree to disagree. But according to the Link and Phelian definition of stigma, it states, "stigma exists when the following interrelated components converge. In the first component, people distinguish and label human differences. In the second, dominant cultural beliefs link labeled persons to undesirable characteristics—to negative stereotypes. In the third, labeled persons are placed in distinct categories so as to accomplish some degree of separation of "us" from "them." In the fourth, labeled persons experience status loss and discrimination that lead to unequal outcomes. Finally, stigmatization is entirely contingent on access to social, economic, and political power that allows the identification of differentness, the construction of stereotypes, the separation of labeled persons into distinct categories, and the full execution of disapproval, rejection, exclusion, and discrimination." Link and Phelian propose that stigma is a layered concept, these layers overlapping and coexisting with one another to make it be defined as "stigma." the first layer being us humans noticing differences from ourselves to others (e.g., "that guy has black hair and i have blonde hair"). The second layer is a cultural significance that impacts the way we perceive certain things (e.g., many regions of the world use different ways to greet each other due to different moral and cultural values). The third layer is a way of categorizing people based on their characteristics, and differentiating them from you yourself (e.g., "I'm part of LGBTQ+, but they are not," "They are a part of the Black community and I am a part of the Hispanic community"). The fourth layer is based on human experiences and how the discrimination they face affects them and labels them as a whole. And lastly, the fifth layer is a layer contributing to many other broad layers. In the end, stigma is a system of overlapping layers that are connected and can have a huge toll on someone's health, life, and overall social status.

Cerebral Palsy Stigma and Discrimination

Cerebral Palsy Is Contagious

A stigma/stereotype of cerebral palsy is that it can be contagious. This is not true and even though there is data and real logical evidence to prove, there is still a stigma lurking around that states that cerebral palsy can be passed along if you come close to someone who has it. Cerebral palsy is mostly developed due to damage in the developing brain (when the baby is a fetus), so this can NOT be true. This stereotype often hurts people with cerebral palsy, it makes them feel isolated due to the fact that many people view their condition as an illness that can be spread around.

People With CP Are Intellectually Disabled

The stigma that people with CP are not capable of learning and not being to be "intelligent" enough, is highly active and hurtful. Due to this view on them, many are demotivated and struggle with self-worth when others put the idea into their heads that they are incapable of having intelligence. This can cause many mental health issues, including depression and anxiety.

People with cerebral have a short-life span

The lifespan for someone with cerebral palsy varies, but those with better healthcare access tend to live longer lives than others. Condemning people with cerebral palsy as sick and with short-life spans heightens the risk of depression and loss of identity.

People with cerebral palsy always are in wheelchairs

There are different types of cerebral palsy diagnoses and each person varies. Some may have better mobility skills than others, while the latter can have worse mobility needs. This is a negative stereotype and can affect them through the lenses of discrimination and prejudice.

A study conducted in 2011 shows that "restrictions in the socio-contextual environment influenced the social exclusion that children experienced. Youth encountered social exclusion from both teachers and peers. Children reported that teachers' attitudes toward children with disabilities often influenced the social exclusion experienced by peers. Bullies engaged in both implicit and explicit forms of social exclusion toward children with disabilities which often lead to verbal and physical bullying." Bullying has always been around, it is a behavior in which kids learn at a young age due to societal factors and differences they find in each other, it is also due to parental figures displaying such actions which later, kids start to mimic. Kids and/or adults are more vulnerable to bullying and discrimination due to the fact that they have a condition that prevents them from being a "normal" person.

Some reasons why kids with cerebral palsy get bullied is because they stand out more often, bullies often think they are vulnerable and cannot defend themselves, they often are deemed socially irrelevant due to their condition, and because certain kids have certain needs, which makes them "easy" targets. Bullying can be from other kids, but also teachers. Bullying isn't always physical, it can also be emotional and psychological, but all these different types of bullying can affect a child's development and can cause severe trauma, leading to forms of mental health disorders taking shape as the child grows up. "Children with CP had higher prevalence of depression (7.8%, 2.7%; p=0.017), anxiety (30.2%, 6.2%; p<0.001), behavior/conduct problems (27.3%, 4.9%; p<0.001), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (19.5%, 7.1%; p=0.030), and multimorbidity (22.3%, 5.2%; p<0.001) compared to controls."

Besides bullying in kids and teens, discrimination is still faced in adult life. Common places of discrimination and stigma are most commonly found in public places. "Of 48 people who answered the quantitative questions a total of 87.5% agreed that they had experienced stigma (62.5% fully agree and 25% mainly agree). Participants (n ranging from 41 to 47) also answered questions on the sources of stigmatisation or discrimination. The four most frequently reported sources of multiple instances of stigma were the public (80%), classmates (57.8%), healthcare professionals (52.2%) and employers (46.3%)."

In adults, substance abuse and mental health conditions worsen. A person with cerebral palsy states, "I do not really drink but I started drinking because it is the cheapest thing I can afford and it's the easiest way I can escape." Depression is most frequent in cases of cerebral palsy due to the fact that they face a lot of discrimination, "Depression and anxiety were frequently reported among participants, though many lacked a formal diagnosis and consequently lacked support. One poignant example is Joy's experience: She spoke extensively about her inability to access services and how this influenced her mental health and well‐being. She experienced fatigue and mental health ramifications associated with CP but was unable to access services and funding because she had "mild" CP and lacked a formal mental health diagnosis. Joy also shared her experience of homelessness."

Transgender Stigma in the United States

Transgender stigma and discrimination in the United States is unfortunately an extremely common occurrence. A study by Jamie M. Grant, a PhD researcher shares how transgender individuals have encountered many types of unwelcoming environments and pressure in their everyday lives.

Discrimination against Transgender People Due to Lack of Acceptance In the Workplace

One of the ways transgender individuals face stigma is at their jobs. The reason for this is there are people who still have yet to accept people who identify with being transgender. These employers may view these individuals as "unprofessional" and this causes gender non-conforming individuals to be removed from jobs or receive less benefits as cisgender people. A study by Jamie M. Grant, a PhD researcher shares, "Forty-seven percent (47%) said they had experienced an adverse job outcome, such as being fired, not hired or denied a promotion because of being transgender…"Grant, J. M. (et al., 2011).

This means that almost half of the people who took this survey who identified with being transgender were outcasted in the workplace because of their gender identity. This negatively affects the lives of transgender individuals because this study reveals that gender non-conforming people are limited to job positions in the work industry due to the prejudice of employers in the workplace.

Discrimination against Transgender Individuals While In Educational Settings

Another way gender non-conforming individuals experience stigma is while pursuing their education. The reason for this could be because maybe other people view transitioning as "abnormal" and different from the gender expectations members of society set in place. This makes transgender people targets for bullying, harassment, exclusion, and violence. For example, "Those who expressed a transgender identity or gender non-conformity while in grades K-12 reported alarming rates of harassment (78%), physical assault (35%) and sexual violence (12%); harassment was so severe that it led almost one-sixth (15%) to leave a school in K-12 settings or in higher education" Grant, J. M. (et al., 2011).

According to this survey conducted by Grant demonstrates high amounts of stigma against transgender individuals. Unfortunately, the participants from the survey have experienced many injustices in the school system with identifying as gender non-conforming. This directly affects these people unfavorably since these injustices could cause transgender people to struggle with their mental health, lower their self-esteem, and struggle more to focus on their educational careers from early on.

Healthcare Access for Cerebral Palsy and Transgender Individuals

Healthcare access for persons with cerebral palsy and transgender individuals is influenced by more than just physiological and psychological needs; it is also shaped by social, attitudinal, and systemic issues. Using a sociological approach, these challenges can be explained through social constructionism and the idea of socially constructed “normal” bodies and identities.

Healthcare Access and Cerebral Palsy

The healthcare system is often inaccessible for people with cerebral palsy in terms of both physical access and quality of care. There are several barriers, including lack of necessary equipment, inaccessible infrastructure, and providers focusing more on the impairment rather than treating the individual as a whole person. As a result, individuals may experience unequal or inadequate treatment.

Social Model of Disability and Healthcare

The social model of disability helps explain these healthcare barriers. According to this model, disability is caused not only by biological factors but also by social and environmental barriers. These barriers can include systems that prevent people from accessing healthcare freely, such as lack of accommodations for different needs and inaccessible facilities.

Healthcare Access and Transgender Individuals

Transgender individuals also face significant barriers in healthcare settings. These include discrimination, lack of provider knowledge, and exclusion within medical systems that often assume patients are strictly male or female. As a result, transgender people may experience delayed care, misgendering, or avoidance of healthcare services altogether.

Theories

Intersectionality

Intersectionality is the different social and political factors of someone’s identity and how it correlates with their experiences and perspectives of discrimination or privileges. There’s three types of levels to intersectionality which are: Inclusion/Voice models, Relational/ Process model, and Systematic/ Anticategorial. The inclusion/voice model mainly focuses on disadvantaged groups and voicing their perspectives and experiences. For example, a Black single mother who is living in a low-income community where she needs certain resources to feed her family, they would use this to share the struggles of this mother The social category of being a single Black mother in a low-income community represents the inclusion/voice model. The relational/process model is a “structural type process-centered analysis” considers the transformations that occur when different statuses meet. Rather than seeing gender and race as additively affecting a person’s experience, they consider both how gender is raced, and race is gendered . systematic/ anticategorial –A final approach may be a fully intersectional model, which does not see any category as more salient than another. Rather, the statuses and their relationships with each other are problematized under the assumption that they continually and mutually constitute each other

Social Constructionism

Definition

Social constructionism is the theory that different parts of identity, including gender and disability, are not completely biological or natural but are created and shaped by society. It explains how ideas of what is considered “normal” or “abnormal” are formed by cultural beliefs, institutions, and historical context rather than fixed truths.

Examples of Theory

Social constructionism helps explain disability as something that is shaped by society and not just biology. D’Souza (2020) argues that the exclusion of children with disabilities in schools is not simply caused by their medical condition, but by “exclusionary practices inherent to schooling.” This means schools can create barriers through systems such as lack of accessibility, rigid academic expectations, and institutional bias. The author also shows how ableism is reinforced through education systems that normalize exclusion and competition, making disabled students more likely to be labeled as “less capable.”

This connects to cerebral palsy because many of the challenges individuals face are not only due to the condition itself, but also because of how schools and society are structured. It also connects to transgender identity because both groups are marginalized when they do not fit what society and education systems consider “normal.”

Nagoshi et al. (2012) found that transgender individuals often challenge traditional heteronormative beliefs about gender roles, gender identity, and sexual orientation. In interviews with self-identified transgender participants, all respondents described gender roles as socially constructed rather than biologically fixed, and viewed gender identity as more fluid than traditional binary ideas of male and female. Many also explained that sexual orientation is closely connected to gender identity and can change depending on how individuals understand themselves.

These findings show that gender is not biologically fixed but shaped through social and cultural influences. This connects to social constructionism because it demonstrates that categories like “male” and “female” are maintained by society rather than nature. It also supports queer theory by challenging heteronormative beliefs and showing that identities outside traditional categories are valid.

How it Correlates with Cerebral Palsy and Transgender Identity

The social constructionist theory argues that the difficulties encountered by people with cerebral palsy and transgender individuals stem from society’s views rather than biological facts alone. Challenges faced by people with cerebral palsy often come from environments that are not designed to accommodate disabled individuals, rigid education systems, and low societal expectations. This suggests that disability is partly created through social structures.

For transgender individuals, difficulties arise from rigid societal expectations about gender roles that people are expected to follow. When individuals do not align with male or female norms, they may face discrimination in schools, healthcare systems, and broader society.

According to Nagoshi et al. (2012), many transgender individuals view gender roles and identity as socially constructed rather than natural or fixed concepts. Overall, both cases demonstrate how “normal” is socially constructed and how these constructions can lead to discrimination and exclusion.

Queer and Crip Theory

Definition:

Queer theory has been defined since the 1990s, said to be a framework which helps understand and examine society’s definitions of gender identity and sexuality. It helps battle against the societal norm of having to be hetrosexual and cis-gender. Developed by philosopher Michel Foucault, he explores and determines the idea that sexuality and gender identity aren’t inherently biological, but are the results of social constructs that shape and help form the way we perceive society and the world. Queer AND Crip Theory is a political, scholarly, and cultural framework that combines queer studies and disability studies to battle against the “Normality” of able-bodied and heterosexual existence.

Examples of theory:

Examples of theory: In the book Dude, You’re a Fag the author, CJ Pascoe, he conducted an ethnographic study on high school teens, to see the dynamic and the way the students perceived one another. “What emerged was a system of students policing one another’s behaviours based on the gender and sexuality related components perceived in their behaviour.” For example, when a guy acted in any way that seemed “feminine,” they would be criticized and called the slur “fag,” due to presenting emotions, proper hygiene, and caring for one’s appearance. This was at no means correlated to their sexuality, this was caused by societal factors. Society has presented these things as something a female would do, making it not “normal” for a guy. Things as basic as good hygiene are now being leveled down to something only girls do, and when a guy does it, it is socially unacceptable. This shows how societal norms and stereotypes cause harm and discrimination, and that labeling something through gender-based norms, can cause more harm than good. Another example, when Pascoe interviewed a cheerleader; “Much as they did with boys, youth at River High associated girls’ gender non-normativity with same-sex desire. When I explained to them that I was “writing a chapter on girls who do guy things,” Sarah (the aforementioned cheerleader) asked, “Oh, you mean lesbians?” This demonstrates the normality of gender-based expectations, and how it is often tied to sexual orientation. When a girl does something a man “would do,” they are often labeled as lesbians due to the societal norm of lesbians being manly, when not all lesbians are masculine.

How it correlates with cerebral palsy and transgender identity:

Queer and Crip Theory both help explain how transgender identity and cerebral palsy are judged based on societal views of what is “normal” and what isn't. In a lot of societies and cultures there is an expectation that people should be cisgender and fit within traditional gender categories, just as there is an expectation that people should be able to fully use their bodies and be physically "typical". When someone doesn't fit either of those norms they are considered different and abnormal.

Queer theory challenges the idea that gender is fixed or purely biological, while Crip theory challenges the idea that disability is just a medical problem that needs to be fixed. Together, they show that a lot of the problems that transgender individuals with cerebral palsy face aren't caused only by their identity or condition, but by social systems that exclude and stigmatize them.

For example a transgender person with cerebral palsy may experience discrimination in healthcare settings where providers may focus only on their disability and ignore their gender identity, or vice versa. They may also face assumptions that they are incapable of making decisions about their own body or identity.

Queer and Crip Theory highlights how these biases are socially created rather than natural, and how institutions often reinforce the idea that only cisgender and able-bodied identities are “normal.” This theory helps connect both experiences by showing how society marginalizes people who fall outside of these expectations.

Social Model of Disability

Definition

The social model of disability is a theory that explains disability as something created by social, environmental, and institutional barriers, rather than the individual's impairment. It separates impairment from disability and argues that people are disabled due to society’s failure to accommodate differences through exclusion, discrimination, and inaccessible structures.

Examples of Theory

An example of the social model of disability is a student who uses a wheelchair being unable to attend classes because of inaccessibility in a school building. In this case, the disability is not the student’s condition itself, but the school’s lack of accessible design that creates barriers and excludes them from the same opportunities as other students.

How it Correlates with Cerebral Palsy and Transgender Identity

The social model of disability connects to cerebral palsy because many of the challenges people face come from discrimination and lack of accessibility rather than the condition itself. For example, if a person with cerebral palsy enters a public space without ramps or elevators, they are excluded due to environmental barriers rather than their impairment.

It also connects to transgender identity because exclusion can occur through social attitudes and institutional systems. For example, if a transgender person is misgendered or denied access to inclusive healthcare or school environments, these barriers are created by society’s judgments rather than the individual’s identity.

Overall, the social model highlights that disability and exclusion are not just medical issues, but are produced and reinforced by how society is designed and how people are treated within it.
